Brave New World

by Aldous Huxley

Published: 1932 Genre: Science Fiction Pages: 311

About This Book

Brave New World envisions a future where humanity has been perfected — and, in the process, destroyed. In the World State of 632 A.F. (After Ford), babies are grown in bottles, genetically engineered for their caste, and conditioned to love their servitude. There is no war, no poverty, no disease, and no freedom. Huxley's dystopia is not one of deprivation but of excess — a world drowning in comfort, entertainment, and the soma pill.

"But I don't want comfort. I want God, I want poetry, I want real danger, I want freedom, I want goodness. I want sin."

Synopsis

In the Central London Hatchery and Conditioning Centre, the Director gives students a tour, explaining how embryos are decanted, sorted into castes — from Alpha intellectuals to Epsilon laborers — and conditioned through hypnopaedia (sleep-teaching) to accept their role and love consumption.

Bernard Marx, an Alpha-Plus who feels like an outsider due to a suspected error in his conditioning, travels to the Savage Reservation in New Mexico, where he finds John — the son of a woman from the World State who was accidentally left behind. John, raised on Shakespeare and indigenous ritual, is brought back to London as a curiosity.

John — "the Savage" — is initially dazzled by the bright new world but soon recoils in horror at its soulless pleasure-seeking. His confrontation with Mustapha Mond, one of the World Controllers, is the philosophical heart of the novel: a debate between happiness and truth, stability and freedom, comfort and meaning.

Chapter 1 — Excerpt

A squat grey building of only thirty-four stories. Over the main entrance the words, CENTRAL LONDON HATCHERY AND CONDITIONING CENTRE, and, in a shield, the World State's motto, COMMUNITY, IDENTITY, STABILITY.

The enormous room on the ground floor faced towards the north. Cold for all the summer beyond the panes, for all the tropical heat of the room itself, a harsh thin light glared through the windows, hungrily seeking some draped lay figure, some pallid shape of academic goose-flesh, but finding only the glass and nickel and bleakly shining porcelain of a laboratory.

Key Themes

Freedom vs. Happiness: The World State has eliminated suffering — but only by eliminating depth. Shakespeare, solitude, love, grief: all are sacrificed on the altar of stability. Huxley asks whether happiness without meaning is worth having.

Technology & Control: Where Orwell imagined control through pain, Huxley imagined it through pleasure. Genetic engineering, pharmacology, and media are the World State's tools — far more effective than any policeman.

Consumerism & Distraction: "Ending is better than mending" — citizens are conditioned to consume, to discard, and never to be alone with their thoughts. The parallels to modern consumer culture are uncomfortably clear.

"Most human beings have an almost infinite capacity for taking things for granted."
