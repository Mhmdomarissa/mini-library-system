The Great Gatsby

by F. Scott Fitzgerald

Published: 1925 Genre: Classic Literature Pages: 180

About This Book

The Great Gatsby is F. Scott Fitzgerald's masterpiece — a slender, shimmering novel that captures the promise and corruption of the American Dream in the Jazz Age. Through the eyes of narrator Nick Carraway, we witness the spectacular rise and devastating fall of Jay Gatsby, a self-made millionaire whose extravagant parties and obsessive love for Daisy Buchanan mask a profound loneliness.

"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."

Synopsis

In the summer of 1922, Nick Carraway moves to New York and rents a modest house on Long Island next to a mysterious mansion. His neighbor, Jay Gatsby, throws lavish parties every weekend — but no one seems to know who he truly is or where his wealth comes from.

Nick soon discovers that Gatsby's spectacle has a singular purpose: to recapture the love of Daisy Buchanan, Nick's cousin, who lives across the bay with her brutish, wealthy husband Tom. The green light at the end of Daisy's dock becomes the symbol of Gatsby's impossible dream — to repeat the past and reclaim a love five years gone.

As Nick is drawn into Gatsby's orbit, he witnesses the collision between old money and new, idealism and corruption, love and selfishness. The summer builds toward a shattering confrontation at the Plaza Hotel and a tragic chain of events that exposes the hollowness behind the golden façade of the American Dream.

Chapter 1 — Excerpt

In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I've been turning over in my mind ever since.

"Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone," he told me, "just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had."

He didn't say any more, but we've always been unusually communicative in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more than that.

Key Themes

The American Dream: Gatsby embodies both the promise (reinvention, ambition, hope) and the betrayal (materialism, illusion, moral vacancy) at the heart of America's founding myth.

Time & Memory: Gatsby's fatal conviction that he can "repeat the past" gives the novel its tragic power. Fitzgerald suggests that the very intensity of desire traps us in time.

Wealth & Class: The contrast between Gatsby's gaudy West Egg and the Buchanans' refined East Egg reveals that in America, not all money is equal. Old wealth despises new wealth, and both crush those without any.

"Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us."
